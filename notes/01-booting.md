## Why are we building this?

Booting is the most primary procedure, executing by a system when power first hits. This is the first task a kernel (heart of the OS, the communicator between hardware and software ) essentially performs to "wake up" a system, which is not done by a singular program, moreover, it is a relay race between 2 programs, before the baton is finally passed onto the kernel (the one I am aiming to build) . When power first hits the computer, our CPU is partially useless; in the sense it still has all its computing logics intact but the internal info of the system, such as free memory, or inputs, is what it lacks, as the CPU executes what is in the RAM, and RAM being a volatile memory, is empty at power on. The CPU essentially loads info from the disks onto the RAM, but the disks themselves need another program to actually load, much like the chicken and the egg analogy. But what we understand is that all these processes and programs run on software, so, to break the system out of the loop of waiting and initiating its first action to starting up, the CPU is hardcoded to jump to execute a permanent memory location (existing in it initially), which it starts executing, this segment of code is firmware, namely, BIOS or UEFI. The firmware wakes up our hardware, gets the mills and cogs of our system running, accounts for the RAM and ventures in search of something "bootable", which is, our disc from before, the firmware looks for a signature marking among the discs, a permanent bookmark of sorts, finds "the one" and a segment of the code from the disc is loaded in the RAM and executes it; The firmware's share of race is over and the baton is passed to the bootloader, our star in this part. To understand its significance, let us initially understand the weird behavior of the CPU at power ON. Initially, the CPU, during power on is essentially like a mound of dough, this is the real mode (addresses only 1MB of memory), in the sense, like a late 70's system, the CPU has no authorization levels to the programs, no kernel and user level distinction, anything can touch anything, also, since it addresses only 1MB of memory at a time, segmented addressing was very very common, leading to the usage of pointers (segment + offset, compared to the current unary pointers used) just to keep track of data, something that doesn't exist for us anymore, because of the size of addressability of protected mode. This is only there to ensure backwards compatibility. The CPU's, nay, the architecture of the entire system, initially was written a long time ago, we, have since then just added on a new layer of tech these coming years, but the soul of the system is vintage, the initial state needs to match that as well, but, before the system actually does anything meaningful, it has to enter a protected mode (user and kernel modes; addresses 4 GB of memory), this work for us, in this kernel development specifically, will be handled by GRUB, which is the real MVP, without which, this project would have had the initial extra week with a time taking detour into assembly (as we would have had to manually address the real mode to be converted into protected mode) + loads the kernel. After the initial step, GRUB will handle the memory and software to us, the kernel, to abide by our bidding, which will be discussed in the coming steps.
